## 1. What do studies show about returns, volatility and sector rotation after midterms (midterm-year pattern, divided government), and how robust are the findings?

### Takeaway
Over long samples, US stocks usually rose in the 12 months after a midterm. The S&P composite was up after 32 of 38 midterms since 1874, with an average gain of about 12–14% depending on the study. The pre-election midterm year tends to be weaker than the post-election year. Divided government, especially under a Republican president (the 2027 configuration), has the best historical averages. The statistics are fragile, though:
- Samples are 15–38 observations in total, and as few as 7–14 per party-control cell.
- The party/returns link does not replicate outside the US.
- Persistent regime dummies inflate statistical significance.

One peer-reviewed study argues the US presidential-party premium is not purely data mining.

### Cited Findings
**Base rates (historical, labeled by period)**
- 1874–2022, 38 midterms, Shiller data: the S&P 500/composite rose in the 12 months after 32 of 38 midterms (~84%), with an average gain of ~14%. — [Stacker/Motley Fool, syndicated Oct 8, 2026 (WDBO)](https://www.wdbo.com/news/midterms-are-coming/YIJA3N4RBM42DHBC5HCXE7326Y/); [Motley Fool Research](https://www.fool.com/research/midterm-election-year-stock-market-history/)
- Same dataset, split by control: divided government averaged a 16% 12-month return and was positive 91% of the time (23 elections). Unified government averaged 12% and was positive 73% of the time (15 elections). — [Motley Fool Research](https://www.fool.com/research/midterm-election-year-stock-market-history/)
- Divided government with a Republican president was the most common arrangement (14 elections): average 18%, positive 93% of the time. Unified Republican control is the only arrangement with a negative average 12-month return (−1%, 7 elections), and three of those seven came right before financial panics. — [Motley Fool Research](https://www.fool.com/research/midterm-election-year-stock-market-history/); [Stacker/WDBO](https://www.wdbo.com/news/midterms-are-coming/YIJA3N4RBM42DHBC5HCXE7326Y/)
- Size of the seat swing: landslide losses for the president's party were not followed by weaker returns than narrow losses. — [Stacker/WDBO](https://www.wdbo.com/news/midterms-are-coming/YIJA3N4RBM42DHBC5HCXE7326Y/)
- U.S. Bank review of 31 midterms: the S&P 500 averaged 12.4% in the 12 months after the election, versus 2.9% in the 12 months before. — [The Berkshire Edge, May 11, 2026, citing U.S. Bank](https://theberkshireedge.com/?p=216649)
- Hartford-attributed analysis (probably Hartford Funds), since 1970: a 6.5% average gain in the 3 months after Election Day, versus 2.5% in the 3 months before. — [Landmark Wealth Management](https://landmarkwealthmgmt.com/?p=2809)
- 2026 pre-election coverage said the S&P 500 was higher one year after every midterm "going back to the early 1980s." This is the excerpt's wording; other sources often cite longer streaks, which I did not verify. — [CNBC, Nov 3, 2026](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/03/midterm-election-stock-market.html)
- Midterm-year drawdowns: BTIG's Jonathan Krinsky noted that the S&P 500 has averaged a ~14% drawdown at some point in the second half of midterm years (pre-election 2026 comment). — [CNBC, Nov 3, 2026](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/03/midterm-election-stock-market.html) (exact source uncertain; may be [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/midterm-election-stocks-market-wall-street/))
- Recent midterm years were weak: 2018 (−4.4%) and 2022 (−18.1%) were the S&P 500's weakest calendar years since 2008. — [BlackRock, "Midterm Elections and Stock Market Trends" (2026)](https://www.blackrock.com/us/financial-professionals/insights/2026-midterm-elections-and-market-performance)
- Sector rotation: per a secondary summary of BlackRock data, the gap between the best and worst sector averaged 11.3% in midterm years since 1990, and sector leadership "rarely followed a party script." Energy was the worst sector under Obama and Trump's first term but among the best under Biden and Trump's second. — [BlackRock](https://www.blackrock.com/us/financial-professionals/insights/2026-midterm-elections-and-market-performance); [multibagg.ai summary](https://www.multibagg.ai/market-pulse/articles/us-midterm-sector-winners-losers-cmw1o73c5000zew2rmay9l24y) (the 11.3% figure and how the gap is defined are not verified)
- Healthcare in midterm years, Thomson Reuters data from 1990 (Reuters story from late 2017): the sector's annual return beat the S&P 500 by 2.7 percentage points on average. — [Gulf Times/Reuters](https://www.gulf-times.com/story/575167/us-healthcare-draws-election-year-worry-but-16-repeat-not-seen)
- Realized volatility: Cboe data cited in a JPMorgan-related report say realized volatility in midterm-election years since 1945 was ~80% higher than in the prior year. The source is machine-translated, and its date and definitions are unverified. — [Webull News (JPMorgan)](https://www.webull.com/news/15656884897776640)

**Robustness and critiques**
- Research Affiliates tested the US link between party in power and returns in five other markets and found no systematic relationship. It argues that many documented relationships reflect reporting bias, data mining and selection bias. — [Research Affiliates](https://www.researchaffiliates.com/publications/articles/614-presidential-politics-and-stock-returns-is-the-relation-real-or-spurious); [ETF.com summary](https://www.etf.com/sections/news/research-affiliates-presidential-politics-stock-returns-relation-real-or-spurious)
- CXO Advisory makes three points:
  - Presidential term-cycle tests rest on only 22, 17 or 36 presidential terms, depending on the sample.
  - A combined calendar/presidential/decennial model did not usefully predict returns.
  - Wong & McAleer's 2008 spectral study found a cycle but over-interprets a small sample.
  - Sources: [CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/calendar-effects/3-cycle-prediction-engine); [CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/?p=1941)
- Statistical method: persistent regime dummy variables (party in power) undermine standard OLS significance tests and overstate regime effects. — [Paper: "Political regimes, business cycles, seasonalities and returns" (PDF)](https://typeset.io/pdf/political-regimes-business-cycles-seasonalities-and-returns-e50a3fcvxt.pdf)
- A practitioner critique (Jan 2012) counts only ~27 presidential terms since 1900, below a common rule of thumb of at least 30 observations. — [Interactive Advisors (Sizemore)](https://investing.interactiveadvisors.com/2012/01/the-presidential-cycle-is-rubbish)
- Counterpoint: Sy & Al Zaman (Journal of Empirical Finance, 2020, vol. 56, pp. 94–104) argue the presidential premium is not a data-mining artifact. It persists after publication, and over two-thirds of it is unexpected rather than spurious-regression bias. — [RePEc/EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:empfin:v:56:y:2020:i:c:p:94-104)
- Practitioner caveats:
  - Outliers such as recessions and panics drive the averages.
  - Studies use different measurement windows and indices (S&P 500 vs Dow, price vs total return).
  - Party control alone doesn't reliably predict returns.
  - Source: [Landmark Wealth Management](https://landmarkwealthmgmt.com/articles/sp-500-midterm-election-performance-why-stocks-have-historically-rallied-after-midterms/)

### Inferences
- The Motley Fool cells are internally consistent with its headline figures:
  - 21 of 23 divided cases (91%) plus 11 of 15 unified cases (73%) gives 32 of 38 positive.
  - (23×16% + 15×12%)/38 ≈ 14.4%, matching the ~14% headline.
- Backing out the remaining cells from those rounded numbers, the error bands are wide:
  - Divided government under a Democratic president (9 cases) averaged roughly 11–15%.
  - Unified Democratic government (8 cases) averaged roughly 20–23%.
  - So "divided beats unified" mostly reflects 7 unified-Republican cases, three of them pre-panic, rather than a clean divided-government effect.
- The 2027 configuration (Republican president, Democratic Congress) sits in the historically best cell (18% average, 13 of 14 positive). With n=14, one more negative case would cut the hit rate to 86%. This is a weak prior, not a signal.
- Part of the "post-midterm rally" is probably mechanical. Midterm years have often had large intra-year drawdowns (BTIG ~14%; 2018 and 2022 down years), so post-election returns are often measured from a depressed base. The 12-month windows also overlap with unrelated macro shocks.
- The failure to replicate outside the US is the strongest reason for skepticism. Sy & Al Zaman's defense covers the party of the president, not the midterm or divided-government effect specifically.

### Gaps
- Primary midterm-cycle studies from LPL, Strategas, Ned Davis, S&P Dow Jones Indices, Goldman or JPMorgan did not surface (fetch blocked; searches returned secondary syndications). An example is the often-cited gain from the midterm-year low to one year later.
- Sample definitions for the Motley Fool and U.S. Bank figures were not verified: price vs total return, and whether the window starts on Election Day or at month-end.
- I did not establish whether 2026 had a typical midterm-year drawdown before the vote.
- Survivorship and backfill: pre-1957 "S&P" history is a reconstructed series (Cowles/Shiller). This is not verified in-session but matters for very long samples.

## 2. In past shifts (2006, 2010, 2018), how did the previous majority's "favored" sectors perform afterward, and how long did any reversal last?

### Takeaway
Coverage from the time shows that day-one reactions centered on healthcare/pharma and defense. The direction depended on what the new majority could realistically enact:
- **2006** (Democrats won both chambers under a Republican president, the closest analog to 2026): drugmakers and defense stocks fell on day one, while the Dow closed at a record.
- **2018** (Democrats won only the House): health insurers and drugmakers rallied, because ACA repeal risk faded and a split Congress limited drug-pricing risk.

No source in this session measured how long these reversals lasted, and I found no sector data for 2010.

### Inferences
- The previous majority's "favored" sectors do not reliably reverse after a midterm. Markets seem to price what the new configuration can actually do:
  - When the new congressional majority faces a president of the other party (2006, 2026), headline risk gets priced, such as drug pricing and defense scrutiny, but the veto limits what can be enacted.
  - A split Congress (2018) was read as protecting the incumbent party's earlier policy wins, such as the ACA.
- The 2006 day-one moves were small next to later macro drivers. Background, not verified in-session: the 2007–08 credit crisis dominated the next 12 months. That fits BlackRock's view that sector leadership rarely follows a party script.
- XBI launched in 2006, so "the last four midterms" most likely means 2010, 2014, 2018 and 2022 (my inference). That is n=4 with a six-month window, which is very thin.
- 2006 is the closest analog to 2026: a Democratic sweep under a Republican president, with war-related scrutiny of defense. Background: Iraq in 2006; for 2026, Morningstar cites "greater scrutiny of the Iran war." In 2006, defense's day-one dip came with a Goldman view that budgets would not fall.

## 3. How do implied and realized volatility usually behave before and after US elections, including for bitcoin?

### Takeaway
Equity implied volatility typically builds into Election Day, mostly in the shortest tenors, and is released quickly after a clear result. 2026 followed that pattern:
- The one-day VIX rose almost 30% into the vote, and the VIX then fell ~18.6% from 20.84 on Nov 4.
- Evidence that the post-election "vol crush" persists beyond a few weeks is weak, and midterm years have historically carried elevated realized volatility.
- Bitcoin's 2026 midterm premium was small next to 2024: forward implied volatility for the election window was ~37.8%, versus DVOL ~63% on the eve of the 2024 vote.
- Bitcoin's DVOL fell from 40.89 to 37.03 after the 2026 vote.

### Cited Findings
**Equities, historical**
- JPMorgan strategists: volatility usually peaks about a month before Election Day and falls back after the election. — [Webull News (JPMorgan; machine-translated, date of note unclear)](https://www.webull.com/news/15656884897776640)
- Cboe data (as cited there): realized volatility in midterm-election years since 1945 was ~80% higher than in the prior year. Unverified. — [Webull News](https://www.webull.com/news/15656884897776640)
- One practitioner analysis: the VIX tends to stabilize within ~30 days after an election and return to normal within ~60 days. — [B&S Capital Markets](https://www.bscapitalmarkets.com/us-election-aftershocks-trading-the-vixrsquos-mean-reversion.html) (attribution within the excerpt is uncertain)
- Skeptical view: a SimianX study of nine midterms (1990–2022) found the post-election VIX crush real but unreliable. It called the one-month crush "mostly folklore" and said the three-month decline looks like ordinary mean reversion from elevated levels. — [SimianX (German-language version)](https://www.simianx.ai/de/stories/vix-after-midterm-elections-1990-2022) (low-authority source, read only in non-English versions)
- After the 2018 midterms, Bloomberg coverage described the VIX as showing "post-midterm investor relief." — [BloombergQuint](https://www.bloombergquint.com/markets/well-that-s-over-with-vix-shows-post-midterm-investor-relief) (headline only)

**Equities, 2026**
- Pre-election, a Reuters analysis (Sep 9, 2026) described a "fearless" US stock market vulnerable to shocks as the midterms loomed. Late-August 2026 reports said traders were hedging against midterm swings. — [Kitco/Reuters, Sep 9, 2026](https://www.kitco.com/news/off-the-wire/2026-09-09/fearless-us-stock-market-vulnerable-shocks-midterms-loom); [roic.ai, Aug 25, 2026](https://www.roic.ai/news/midterm-elections-fuel-volatility-fears-as-traders-hedge-against-market-swings-08-25-2026) (headlines only)
- Into the vote, the one-day VIX (VIX1D) jumped almost 30% and closed above every longer tenor, an inverted front end. The same brief cites implied correlation at a record; the excerpt's figure ("16.09") is garbled and unverified. — [Saxo Options Brief, Nov 4, 2026 ("Blue wave meets a one-day premium")](https://www.home.saxo/en-ch/content/articles/options/blue-wave-meets-a-one-day-premium---options-brief---4-november-2026-04112026)

**Bitcoin**
- Block Scholes snapshot, Oct 26, 2026: forward implied volatility for the 7–14-day window containing Election Day was 37.8%, below the 2024 level. Block Scholes cautioned that this annualized figure is neither an expected 37.8% move nor a directional forecast. — [DigitalToday ("Bitcoin options market muted ahead of US midterm elections")](https://www.digitaltoday.co.kr/en/view/121586/bitcoin-options-market-muted-ahead-of-us-midterm-elections); [CryptoSlate](https://cryptoslate.com/?p=577972) (exact attribution within the excerpt is uncertain)
- Nov 2, 2026: bitcoin's implied-volatility term structure showed a bump for expiries just after the midterm. That suggests traders were positioning for a brief burst of turbulence rather than prolonged turmoil. — [Dimsum Daily](https://www.dimsumdaily.hk/?p=649337)
- After the vote, Deribit's DVOL (30-day) fell from 40.89 to 37.03 by Wednesday morning, Nov 4, 2026. — [CryptoTicker, Nov 4, 2026](https://cryptoticker.io/en/bitcoin-price-prediction-volatility-after-election-november-4-2026/) (single source)
- 2024 comparison: DVOL reached 63.24% on the eve of the 2024 presidential vote, its highest since July 2024. — [CoinDesk, Nov 3, 2024](https://hotfix.coindesk.com/markets/2024/11/03/bitcoin-volatility-jumps-to-3-month-high-ahead-of-us-election)
- In 2024, Deribit's CEO said forward implied volatility of 72.29% implied a ~3.78% swing around Election Day, and demand for calls surged in the first expiry after the election. — [The Block, Nov 2024](https://www.theblock.co/amp/post/323053/bitcoin-call-options-see-surge-in-demand-for-first-expiry-after-us-election-deribit-ceo-says); see also [Panoptic research on crypto IV around the 2024 election](https://panoptic.xyz/research/crypto-implied-volatility-election-2024)

### Inferences
- The post-election VIX was about 20.84 × (1 − 0.186) ≈ 17.0.
- DVOL's drop from 40.89 to 37.03 is about −9.4%, a smaller proportional crush than the VIX's −18.6%. That is consistent with a midterm being a smaller crypto catalyst than a presidential race.
- Converting annualized implied volatility with the 365-day crypto convention:
  - 37.8% is roughly a ±2.0% one-day or ±5.2% one-week one-standard-deviation move.
  - The 2024 Deribit figure is consistent with this method: 72.29%/√365 ≈ 3.78%, so that quote was a one-day move.
- The election premium sits in the shortest tenors and is released immediately. Whether volatility stays low afterward depends on policy and macro news rather than the vote itself. An example is the AI-oversight sell-off reported after Nov 4 (see section 4).

### Gaps
- I retrieved no primary Cboe VIX series, so I could not compute the average VIX change from one month before to one and three months after each midterm, or check SimianX's figures.
- Realized versus implied volatility in the 2026 post-election week, and whether the VIX rebounded during the reported tech "rout," are not established.
- I have no primary Deribit DVOL data and did not capture bitcoin's spot reaction to the 2026 vote.

## 5. How have prediction-market and event-contract traders positioned around post-election policy fights (shutdowns, debt limits, impeachment), and how did those markets resolve?

### Takeaway
- **Shutdowns:** in recent fights, Kalshi and Polymarket traders priced long standoffs early, with Kalshi's implied average length at ~44 days in Oct 2025. They converged to near-certainty only close to deadlines (99–100¢ before the Jan 31, 2026 lapse). Contract wording, such as an OPM announcement versus a signing deadline, was a material resolution risk.
- **Impeachment:** markets consistently price House impeachment far above Senate removal. After the Democratic sweep, Trump impeachment before the end of his term traded around 65–66%, with near-dated contracts much lower.
- **Tight deadlines:** past contracts with short windows resolved "No," for example Biden impeached before the 2024 election, and the debt ceiling raised or suspended by inauguration in 2025.

### Inferences
- Kalshi's ~44-day expected duration in Oct 2025 was close to the shutdown's actual length of ~43 days (Oct 1 to Nov 12, 2025; background, not verified in-session). That suggests reasonable duration calibration in that episode, while odds swung heavily as votes approached.
- Markets price the incoming Democratic House's ability to impeach, which needs a simple majority, but keep Senate removal, which needs two-thirds, low. Near-dated contracts such as "before March 2027" are the most sensitive to events, because the new House convenes in January 2027.
- Several tight-deadline "drama" contracts resolved No. Examples are Biden impeachment by Nov 2024 despite a GOP House, and the debt-limit contract by Jan 2025. Timing constraints frequently defeat such outcomes, which matters for contracts pricing a FY2027 shutdown or impeachment within short windows.
- Wording differs across venues (Kalshi, Polymarket, PredictIt), which creates basis risk for anyone hedging the same political event in more than one place.
