## Context: What is the new balance of power (federal and Arizona), and how did markets first react?

### Takeaway
Democrats won both chambers on 2026-11-03. The Senate stood at least 54-44, with Mississippi and Alaska uncalled. The House majority ranges from about 237 to 258 or more seats depending on source and date. The new majorities take power when the 120th Congress is sworn in on 2027-01-03. Democrats lack both 60-vote Senate and veto-proof majorities, so analysts expect change to come mainly through oversight, Senate confirmation power and leverage at fiscal deadlines, not through new laws Trump opposes. In Arizona, Gov. Katie Hobbs and Attorney General Kris Mayes (both Democrats) were re-elected by about 11 to 13 points; Mayes is the official prosecuting Kalshi. On the first trading day (2026-11-04) the market rallied broadly, led by hospitals, Medicaid insurers, solar and utilities. Defense, AI-infrastructure "neoclouds", oil and gas, and private prison operators lagged.

### Inferences
- There are two policy windows. The first is the lame duck through 2027-01-03, still run by Republicans, with the 2026-12-11 funding deadline, the NDAA and possibly the debt limit. The second is the 120th Congress under Democrats from January 2027.
- With no veto-proof margin, new laws Trump opposes are unlikely. Democratic leverage sits in three places: must-pass bills (any FY2027 funding extension into 2027, FY2028 appropriations due 2027-10-01, and the 2027 debt-limit increase); Senate confirmation power over the Fed, SEC, CFTC and judges; and committee subpoenas and oversight.
- Arizona: the re-election of Hobbs and Mayes means no change in state leadership. The AG who brought the Kalshi prosecution has a new four-year term, and the Department of Gaming stays under the same governor.

## Trade and tariffs: what can a changed Congress do, and what are analysts and lawmakers saying now?

### Takeaway
The tariff regime had already changed before the election. On 2026-02-20 the Supreme Court held that IEEPA does not authorize tariffs. The administration replaced those tariffs with a 10% Section 122 surcharge, which the Court of International Trade ruled unlawful in May 2026 in a decision that is not final, and relied more on Sections 232 and 301, which need no vote of Congress. House Democrats plan votes in early 2027 to undo tariffs and take back tariff authority, but the 60-vote Senate threshold and Trump's veto make enactment unlikely. Goldman Sachs and KOTRA expect most tariffs to stay. Goldman expects any rollback to target a few countries (e.g., Canada) or products, won as concessions at fiscal deadlines and in the 2027 debt-limit fight.

### Inferences
- Congress is more likely to change tariffs through concessions in must-pass bills than through a standalone repeal. The realistic leverage points are any FY2027 funding extension that runs into 2027, the 2027 debt-limit increase and FY2028 appropriations (due 2027-10-01). See the oversight and fiscal section for dates.
- Following Goldman's framing, Canada-exposed trade and specific product categories are the likeliest to get relief. Broadly tariff-sensitive sectors such as retail and autos should not expect sweeping relief from Congress, while new Section 232 and 301 actions remain in executive hands.
- Courts remain a separate source of tariff change: the Section 122 appeal and IEEPA refunds, which affect importers' cash flow.

## Crypto: pending legislation (market structure, stablecoins), SEC and CFTC posture, and industry expectations

### Takeaway
The CLARITY Act (market structure) passed the House in July 2025 but stalled in the Senate, where cloture on the motion to proceed failed 49-50 on 2026-09-15. Absent lame-duck action, it dies when the 119th Congress ends on 2027-01-03, and I found no lame-duck vehicle. Democrats, with Warren expected to chair Senate Banking and Waters the House Financial Services Committee, are reportedly preparing a broader bill that gives the SEC a bigger role and adds ethics limits aimed at Trump's crypto interests. TD Cowen sees little chance of legislation in the next two years. SEC Chair Atkins and CFTC Chair Selig say they will write crypto rules without Congress, and lawyers expect the midterms not to slow that much. Risks: a Democratic Senate can block new SEC and CFTC nominees, and rules written by agencies can be reversed later. Stablecoin payments and tokenization are seen as relatively insulated.

## Prediction markets: CFTC and event-contract outlook, state challenges to Kalshi, and Arizona (Ninth Circuit; service to Arizona residents)

### Inferences
- **Arizona, near term.** The Nevada ruling is now final at the circuit level, and the CFTC has conceded Liburdi's reasoning cannot stand. The Ninth Circuit therefore seems likely to vacate or narrow the Arizona injunction as to sports contracts, possibly within weeks to months. That would let the re-elected Mayes revive the sports-related counts. The election-wagering counts (four of 20) are less clear, because the Ninth Circuit carved election contracts out of its Nevada holding.
- **Serving Arizona residents.** If the injunction is lifted, Kalshi would be caught between state prosecution risk, which pushes toward geofencing Arizona for sports, and the CFTC's 2026-11-05 interpretation discouraging state geoblocks. Expect Kalshi to seek a stay pending a Supreme Court petition. Until the Ninth Circuit acts, Kalshi appears able to keep serving Arizona.
- **Supreme Court.** The Third and Ninth Circuits are split, Kalshi has also lost in the Second Circuit, and petitions are already pending (Robinhood, reportedly Crypto.com), with Kalshi's due about 2027-02-02. That raises the odds the Court takes the CFTC-preemption question. When it would rule is speculative.
- **Congress.** Expect 2027 hearings and possible subpoenas touching the CFTC, Selig and Trump Jr.'s ties. An outright federal ban on sports contracts is unlikely to become law while Trump can veto, though narrower insider-trading rules have better odds. A Democratic Senate can also block additional CFTC nominees, which leaves the CFTC's pro-preemption stance in place but under scrutiny.
- **Election contracts** remain the least resolved category, in Arizona and in Nevada after the remand.
