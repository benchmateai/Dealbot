# Kalshi rewards resting orders, not better forecasts

On Kalshi, every automated strategy whose logic holds up makes its money from the same two sources: recreational takers who overpay for long shots, and Kalshi's own payments for resting liquidity. Every one of them works by resting orders rather than by forecasting better than the price. Fifteen strategies pass that test. The strongest is capturing Kalshi's Liquidity Incentive Program, which on November 3 was paying about **$92,000 a day across roughly 4,400 markets** for resting two-sided depth (**35%** confidence of a real, lasting edge after fees). Next is resting bids on the favorite side of long-shot markets (**30%**), then model-anchored quoting in thinner sports, crypto and weather books (**15–20%**), then ten narrower ideas (**8–12%**). No strategy has an audited after-fee record, so the scores rest on how sound each mechanism is rather than on reported returns. In the desk's own markets, the careful tests of forecasting and taking came back null or negative, because the 15-minute BTC and ETH market price is well calibrated and beat every model tried. The desk loses by design: it averages a Coinbase-based formula with an LLM and leaves the market price out of the blend, which drags its forecasts toward 50/50, makes the cheap side of every contract look underpriced, and so buys exactly the long shots on which Kalshi takers lose most. The repair is to start from the market price, give the LLM zero weight until thousands of resolved forecasts justify more, price the real settlement rule (a 60-second average of the CF Benchmarks index), and use the corrected model to price resting quotes instead of taking. Strategies that cross the spread can be paper-tested honestly at the displayed price. Strategies that rest orders, including all five top-ranked ones, yield only an optimistic ceiling on paper and need a full order-book record plus a tiny live pilot before anyone knows whether they make or lose money.

## Every durable edge has a recreational taker or Kalshi itself on the other side

A maker rests an order on the book; a taker crosses the spread and fills against it. Before fees, every Kalshi trade is zero-sum between the two, and Kalshi charges a fee on top. A strategy therefore has a lasting edge only if it can name who loses to it and why that loss outlasts the fee. Two counterparties pass that test.

The first is the recreational taker. Across **72.1 million Kalshi trades** from June 2021 to November 2025, takers averaged an excess return (win rate minus price paid) of about **−1.12%** and makers **+1.12%** before fees. Takers came out negative at 80 of 99 price levels, and takers buying 1¢ contracts won **0.43%** of the time against the 1% the price implied ([Becker](https://www.jbecker.dev/research/prediction-market-microstructure)). Bürgi, Deng and Whelan find that buyers of contracts under 10¢ "lose over 60 percent of their money", and that takers lose almost 32% on average against about 10% for makers ([VoxEU](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/economics-kalshi-prediction-market)). Those are averages of percentage returns dominated by long-shot losses, not dollar P&L, and they do not mean market making loses money. Bartlett traces the makers' profit to traders who "systematically overbet YES in markets that predominantly settle NO" ([Bartlett](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/404044013_Adverse_Selection_in_Prediction_Markets_Evidence_from_Kalshi)).

The second counterparty is Kalshi itself, which pays members to display depth. A professional market maker working from the same information does not count: trading against one is a speed race, or a coin flip minus the fee, and most failed strategies turn out to be exactly that trade.

The fee sets the hurdle. Takers pay 0.07 × contracts × P × (1 − P), where P is the price, which comes to **1.75¢ per contract at 50¢**. A taker buying at 50¢ must therefore win 51.75% of the time, and one buying 100 contracts at 95¢ must win 95.34% ([Kalshi fee schedule](https://kalshi.com/docs/kalshi-fee-schedule.pdf)).

Makers pay nothing except in the **160 of 15,679 series** that charge a maker fee at a quarter of the taker rate. Those series are concentrated in the deepest sports game lines and the Fed, CPI, payrolls and GDP markets. Fifteen-minute and hourly crypto, daily weather, hourly stock-index, election and "mentions" markets charge makers nothing ([Kalshi API](https://api.elections.kalshi.com/trade-api/v2/series)). The fee-charging series are the busy ones, covering 14.4% of markets but about 66% of volume ([Vladosyna replication](https://github.com/Vladosyna/kalshi-makers-takers-persistence)).

Fee rounding is unresolved. Most guides, and the desk's own fee code, round each order's fee up to the cent, under which a one-contract buy at 99¢ can never profit. Kalshi's current documentation reportedly rounds to $0.0001 for members who hold accounts directly ([Kalshi docs](https://docs.kalshi.com/getting_started/fee_rounding)), and a single live fill will settle it.

Kalshi also changes fees series by series in both directions: within 13 months it added maker fees, halved MLB fees and made several long-dated political markets fee-free. Any bot should read each series' fee type and multiplier from the API at runtime rather than hard-code them ([Kalshi API fee log](https://api.elections.kalshi.com/trade-api/v2/series/fee_changes?show_historical=true)).

The transfer from takers to makers is real but uneven, and it runs thinnest where the desk trades. The maker-taker gap is **0.17 points in finance**, against more than 7 points in entertainment and media ([KuCoin summary of Becker](https://www.kucoin.com/news/flash/analysis-reveals-systematic-wealth-transfer-in-prediction-markets)). In 15-minute BTC, favorites priced 80–99¢ roughly earn their price, and only contracts under 5¢ look slightly overpriced ([coloshword](https://github.com/coloshword/kalshi-15m-backtest)).

The maker side only started winning in the second quarter of 2024, once books grew deep enough to attract professionals; before that, takers earned +2.0% ([fatskier replication](https://github.com/fatskier/prediction-marks)). Bürgi and coauthors see the bias weakening ([GWU working paper](https://www2.gwu.edu/~forcpgm/2026-001.pdf)). The makers' aggregate returns also go to the established firms at the front of each price queue, not to a newcomer who joins behind them.

The evidence base deserves a warning. All six research teams behind this report were blocked from most primary web pages, so figures credited to papers and to Kalshi's help pages come from search-engine summaries. The first-hand sources were Kalshi's public API (fee settings, incentive programs, settled markets and price history), its contract-terms files, Coinbase price history, GitHub write-ups, and one researcher's own analysis of 1,904 recent 15-minute contracts.

No strategy has an audited live track record. People selling bots, scanners and hosting dominate the public record. The few efforts that publish their methods, among them the-oracle, coloshword, AllAboutAI-YT, J0shusmc, edgebook and PredictMarketCap, are uniformly null or negative for taker strategies. Several sources also have interests at stake: Kalshi's own calibration and inflation studies are written by the exchange, and the author of the largest trade study works on Coinbase's prediction-markets team ([LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/jon--becker/)).

The scores below are therefore judgments about mechanism. Each is the probability that a strategy has a real edge after Kalshi fees, lasting at least 6–12 months at a small automated desk's scale. The research teams sometimes scored the same mechanism differently: maker long-shot fading drew 25–35% overall and 8% in weather alone. In such cases this report moves toward the low end when support is self-reported and toward the high end when Kalshi's own data backs the mechanism.

## Fifteen strategies pass the logic test, and none tops 35%

The table ranks every strategy that names a counterparty who loses for a lasting reason and that a small automated operator can run.

Evidence quality grades the support for the strategy itself, not for the general bias. **Strong** means large Kalshi datasets back it, **moderate** means primary Kalshi data or several independent tests, **weak** means one small or self-reported test or mixed results, and **anecdotal** means nothing has been measured.

Paper-test honesty runs from high, meaning taker fills at the displayed ask and size on one venue with exact fees, to low, meaning fills on resting orders. In the low case paper produces a ceiling, and the real result could be a loss.

### Getting paid to rest orders leads the list

Kalshi's Liquidity Incentive Program pays ordinary members a pro-rata share of a per-market pool for resting size on both sides near the best prices. Kalshi snapshots each order book once a second, each price step (tick) behind the best price halves an order's weight, and resting orders earn whether or not they trade ([Kalshi Help](https://help.kalshi.com/en/articles/13823851-liquidity-incentive-program)).

At about 19:00 UTC on November 3, Kalshi's API showed **4,385 markets** with live programs accruing about $92,000 a day combined. The median market paid about **$14 a day** and the largest $168, with about $12.5 million of pools scheduled from September 21 to November 30 ([Kalshi API](https://api.elections.kalshi.com/trade-api/v2/incentive_programs)). Those dollar figures assume the API's undocumented reward unit is hundredths of a cent, which cross-checks support. Holders of Kalshi's private Market Maker Agreements are excluded, which keeps the largest professional firms out of the pool, though not out of the fills.

The logic is the soundest on the list, because the counterparty is a marketing budget rather than a trader. The scattered practitioner evidence agrees. An independent search for Kalshi edges concluded that "the one that survives is not a forecast at all: it is the exchange's own liquidity subsidy" ([geniuswang05](https://github.com/geniuswang05/kalshi_alpha_search)). A reward-farming bot reports that its fills lose money on their own while the rewards carry it ([JosephWong333](https://github.com/JosephWong333/prediction-market-bots)). A Polymarket US maker that used Kalshi prices as fair value earned **$52.53 in rebates and $11.36 in liquidity rewards against $4.75 from trading** over 44 days ([Tanuj-07](https://github.com/Tanuj-07/polymarket-market-making)).

The payout formula can be reproduced, so the reward side can be estimated before risking money; one small agent's model matched its real payouts within about 3% ([lip-ledger](https://github.com/mirabilis-agent/lip-ledger)). As a rough illustration, holding a tenth of the scored size in a $14-a-day market earns about $1.40 a day on roughly $100 of collateral. That is about 1% a day before losses on fills.

Three things hold the score at 35%. First, the halving per tick forces quotes to the best price, exactly where informed takers pick them off. This week's pools sit mostly in election-day, "Trump says", Rotten Tomatoes and YouTube-view markets, where news arrives in bursts.

Second, anyone can join, so competition will push the reward down toward what informed traders take from the least-skilled quoter. A former trader at Kalshi's in-house market maker estimates that quoting on and off earns only about a tenth of what steady makers earn, so uptime matters more than speed ([UFO Holdings](https://ufoholdings.substack.com/p/kalshis-new-liquidity-incentives)).

Third, the subsidy is discretionary. Kalshi amended the program at least three times in 2026 ([CFTC filing](https://www.cftc.gov/filings/orgrules/rules07152610358.pdf)) and is ending a separate volume-incentive program amid wash-trading allegations ([The Block](https://theblock.co/news/business/2026-09-30-kalshi-ends-trader-incentive-program-417247)). Kalshi's API lists those volume programs, which ran from April to October 28, mostly on KXBTC15M and KXETH15M, the desk's own series ([Kalshi API](https://api.elections.kalshi.com/trade-api/v2/incentive_programs)). So the one subsidy that touched the desk's markets is ending, and the live liquidity pools are elsewhere.

Maker long-shot fading takes the other side of that "optimism tax". In Becker's data, every price below 20¢ did worse than its odds and every price above 80¢ did better. Takers supplied 41–47% of YES buying at 1–10¢, and makers buying at 1¢ won 1.57% of the time against 1% implied ([Becker](https://www.jbecker.dev/research/prediction-market-microstructure)). Bürgi and coauthors find that makers buying above 50¢ earn statistically significant positive returns ([GWU working paper](https://www2.gwu.edu/~forcpgm/2026-001.pdf)). A replication puts the maker margin above 50¢ at **+2.09%**, with no detectable effect on the bias from the 2025 maker fees ([Vladosyna](https://github.com/Vladosyna/kalshi-makers-takers-persistence)).

Fees barely touch the strategy: the maker fee at 99¢ is 0.017¢ where charged, and zero on most series. The behavior behind it has been documented in horse racing since 1949, which makes it about as persistent as market quirks get ([JSTOR Daily](https://daily.jstor.org/betting-on-the-longshot)).

The score stops at 30% because a newcomer captures only part of it. The measured returns are pooled, counted before fees, and earned by firms at the front of the queue; makers already do 43% of the NO buying at 99¢. A pre-registered out-of-sample test of favorite buying in weather failed, at −0.5% pooled after fees with an interval of −8.0% to +7.1% ([the-oracle](https://github.com/christurgeon/the-oracle/blob/main/docs/research/2026-10-01-favorite-bias-oos-g0-results.md)).

On Polymarket, long shots that lost 6.3¢ per dollar when each contract was counted separately gained 4.1¢ once related contracts were grouped by event ([Cardozo & Rivero-Wildemauwe](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12878)). That is a warning that a ladder of NO bids on one event is one correlated bet, not many. The upset arithmetic is unforgiving: one loss at 95¢ cancels about 20 wins, and one at 99¢ about 106.

The fade belongs where recreational YES buying is heaviest. That means entertainment and media, sports futures and long-shot props, and markets about a single candidate or team, where makers earn twice as much per contract ([Bartlett](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/404044013_Adverse_Selection_in_Prediction_Markets_Evidence_from_Kalshi)). Orders should rest early, not in the final minutes, when a resting 97¢ bid fills precisely because the outcome has turned doubtful. A student paper found favorites overpriced, not underpriced, in the last hours of Polymarket weather markets ([Qiu & Qiu](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13593)).

### Model-anchored quoting in thinner books forms the second tier

Strategies 3 to 5 share one idea: a pricing model that cannot beat the market price as a taker can still earn as a maker. As a maker, the model only has to decide where to quote and when to pull out, and the trader collects the spread instead of paying it plus a fee.

Sports market making has the most recreational money behind it, with Kalshi's sports volume reaching $9.9 billion in March 2026 alone ([Blockworks](https://app.blockworks.com/report/from-betting-to-trading-how-kalshi-is-reshaping-sports-markets)). The headline games are the wrong place, though. Maker fees now cover NFL, NBA and MLB game lines, and one check found that makers in major leagues actually captured **0.02–0.14¢ a contract, against a 0.2–0.4¢ maker fee** ([geniuswang05](https://github.com/geniuswang05/kalshi_alpha_search)). Kalshi's main game markets sit a median 0.75 points from Pinnacle's prices with the bookmaker's margin removed ([OddsPapi](https://oddspapi.io/blog/polymarket-api-kalshi-api-vs-sportsbooks-the-developers-guide/)). They are quoted by Susquehanna and by professional bettors such as Rufus Peabody, who sees "a lot of alpha" there ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-02-05/super-bowl-lx-is-bringing-professional-gamblers-to-prediction-markets)).

The opening is secondary leagues, alternate lines and props, where the sharp feed's main job is telling the bot when to cancel. The one public P&L breakdown shows why that job matters. A Polymarket bot quoted off bookmaker odds with the margin removed and hedged after fills. It made **+$8,293** on the positions it hedged, lost **$3,184** on unhedged "+EV" leftovers, and was retired by its author as "too slow to defend its edge" ([polymm](https://github.com/kachence/polymm)). Fills against a quote anchored to sharp lines tend to arrive just after the fair price has moved.

Fast odds data is the cost. Pinnacle closed its public API on July 23, 2025 ([Pinnacle](https://github.com/pinnacleapi/pinnacleapi-documentation)). The Odds API refreshes its main markets every 40–60 seconds ([The Odds API](https://the-odds-api.com/sports-odds-data/update-intervals.html)), slow enough to mistake your own data lag for Kalshi mispricing, and sub-second feeds cost about $800–5,000 a month ([SportsAPIs.dev](https://sportsapis.dev/apis/unabated); [OddsPapi](https://oddspapi.io/blog/oddsjam-api-alternative/)). Legal access is also in play: on September 25, the Sixth Circuit ruled that states can regulate Kalshi sports contracts ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/25/appeals-court-rules-states-can-regulate-sports-prediction-markets.html)).

Crypto market making is the desk's natural home and its hardest arena. BTC 15-minute markets trade a median **3.11 million contracts per window**, with 1¢ spreads early in the window and 0.1¢ in the last three minutes ([Kalshi API](https://api.elections.kalshi.com/trade-api/v2/markets?series_ticker=KXBTC15M&status=settled)). The competition is institutional quoters, including Susquehanna and Jump ([Kalshi News](https://news.kalshi.com/p/liquid-prediction-markets-are-finally-here); [DeFi Rate](https://defirate.com/news/equity-for-liquidity-jump-trading-set-to-take-stakes-in-kalshi-and-polymarket/)).

ETH trades about 214,000 contracts per window, roughly 14 times fewer. Hourly and daily strikes far from the current price split professional attention across hundreds of prices. Those are the openings, though Kalshi's 15-minute series for coins other than BTC, ETH included, list a designated liquidity provider ([Kalshi Help](https://help.kalshi.com/en/articles/15410219-liquidity-provider-program)).

Makers pay no fee on any of these series ([Kalshi API](https://api.elections.kalshi.com/trade-api/v2/series/KXBTC15M)). One study treats Kalshi and Polymarket crypto contracts like options and backs out the volatility their prices imply. It reports a "robust variance risk premium", a built-in premium that a quoting seller collects ([Lee, Lee & Lee](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1057521926003601)).

Against that, the most candid small-maker report says "almost every edge that looked real in-sample decayed out-of-sample within a few weeks". What survived was a post-only offer on the long-shot side that is "roughly break-even under pessimistic fill assumptions" ([artyomderkach](https://github.com/artyomderkach-bit/kalshi-15m-market-maker)). A Polymarket 15-minute maker stopped making money once it was "at least 100 milliseconds behind participants with direct exchange access" ([QuantumSlayer](https://github.com/QuantumSlayer/poly-15min)).

The Polymarket trick of buying both sides for under $1 is no arbitrage here. In a Kalshi book the NO ask is always 1 minus the YES bid, so on Kalshi the trade is just two-sided market making under another name.

Weather makes the same point from the other direction. Its books are thin, with a median spread of about 3¢ and trading going quiet a median 13 hours before settlement, yet the prices are sharp. Across 776 above-or-below markets the price scored a Brier of 0.071, against 0.199 for the National Blend of Models, the NWS's best combined forecast ([the-oracle](https://github.com/christurgeon/the-oracle/blob/main/docs/research/2026-05-30-weather-edge-search-dossier.md)). The market beat the best single public forecast in 6 of 7 cities and moved before the forecasts did ([Crosier](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.23969)). Three strategies built on standard NWS forecast statistics, tested on 40,032 contracts, found nothing after fees ([OddsReference](https://oddsreference.com/predictions/research/kalshi-weather-model)).

Forecasting is closed off; the maker role and the rulebook remain. Makers pay no fee in weather. Entering as a maker four hours out improved results by 1.5¢ a signal (interval +0.5¢ to +2.6¢), though the strategy's return interval still spanned zero ([the-oracle](https://github.com/christurgeon/the-oracle/blob/main/docs/research/2026-10-01-favorite-bias-oos-g0-results.md)).

The rulebook changed in August, when Kalshi moved daily temperature settlement from the NWS climate report to TWC. TWC checks readings against nearby stations, model analysis and more than 400,000 personal weather stations; sources put the switch on August 12, 14 or 27 ([Claims Journal](https://www.claimsjournal.com/news/national/2026/08/31/339851.htm)). The only systematic weather flaw any public effort has found was a settlement effect. Airport sensor readings ran about 1°F off the official climate report, so low-probability temperature ranges came true about twice as often as sensor-based history predicted ([the-oracle](https://github.com/christurgeon/the-oracle/blob/main/docs/research/2026-06-13-hrrr-intraday-nogo-dossier.md)).

Modeling how TWC's figure differs from the readings most traders watch (strategy 6) is therefore the one weather information edge with a precedent, and the same model is what a weather maker needs. Both edges are temporary, as vendors already sell minute-by-minute settlement-station data ([MinuteTemp](https://minutetemp.com/)). Capacity is tiny, with the median taker able to trade only about $88 a day out of sample, and every piece of evidence predates the switch.

### Narrow taker plays and cheap scanners fill out the tail

Two taker strategies make the list because their logic is sound and paper can judge them honestly. Thin-market sports anchoring (strategy 7) works only where the resting order being taken is a stale recreational order rather than a professional quote. Nothing has measured how often that happens, and sharp reference prices are weaker for props. A vendor study of 600 million MLB prop line moves claims Kalshi posts the sharpest prices of any book ([SmartStake](https://www.smartstake.app/learn/kalshi-vs-sportsbook)), which would make Kalshi the price-setter there. Following the consensus line has paid before, against slow sportsbooks, until the books limited the winners' accounts ([Kaunitz et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.02824)). Kalshi does not limit winners, but its main books are not slow.

Late-window tail harvesting (strategy 8) exploits the settlement rule. The 15-minute contract settles on the simple average of 60 one-second CF Benchmarks index readings before the close, against a strike that is the same average taken before the open ([Kalshi API](https://api.elections.kalshi.com/trade-api/v2/markets?series_ticker=KXBTC15M&status=open)). Inside the final minute part of that average is already fixed. With r seconds left, the remaining uncertainty is σ√(r³/10,800), where σ is per-second volatility. At 30 seconds that is about 3.5 times smaller than a spot-price model assumes, so a contract can be a near-certain YES while spot sits below the strike.

Market makers already price this. One minute out, the market's log loss was 0.183 against 0.253 for the best averaging-aware model, so the edge belongs to whoever updates fastest from the real index. The evidence on late favorites is mixed. The researcher's 95–100¢ buckets ran −0.4¢ to +1.25¢ a contract for BTC and +0.35¢ to +0.80¢ for ETH, over 150–260 cases ([Kalshi API](https://api.elections.kalshi.com/trade-api/v2/markets/candlesticks)). An unverified study of trade records claims +1.90¢ for takers who bought favorites in the final 60 seconds ([predictionmarketspicks](https://predictionmarketspicks.com/articles/kalshi-bitcoin-15-minute-last-60-seconds)). And the contract terms settle affected strikes No if index data is missing ([CRYPTO15M terms](https://kalshi-public-docs.s3.amazonaws.com/contract_terms/CRYPTO15M.pdf)), a small risk for anyone holding YES at 99¢.

Kalshi-only consistency arbitrage (strategy 9) has the most robust logic of any arbitrage, because it needs no forecast. In a set of n outcomes where at most one can happen, owning NO on every leg pays at least n − 1, whether or not one of them happens. So when the YES bids sum above $1 plus fees, buying every NO locks in a profit. Kalshi's opt-in Collateral Return releases most of that guaranteed payout early, though the setting locks in at the first order in an event ([Kalshi Help](https://help.kalshi.com/en/articles/13823816-collateral-return)).

Ladders of "above X" contracts obey the same logic. Whenever the bid for "above Y" exceeds the ask for "above X" with Y the higher number, buying YES on X and NO on Y costs under $1 and pays at least $1.

The desk's own markets hold a pair of this kind that none of the research tested. A 15-minute window that closes on the hour settles on the same 60-second index average as that hour's KXBTCD ladder ([KXBTC15M rules](https://api.elections.kalshi.com/trade-api/v2/markets?series_ticker=KXBTC15M&status=open); [KXBTCD rules](https://api.elections.kalshi.com/trade-api/v2/markets?series_ticker=KXBTCD&status=open)). Suppose the 15-minute YES bid exceeds the ask on an hourly "above K" YES, with K below the 15-minute strike. Then buying the hourly YES and the 15-minute NO pays at least $1 in every outcome, a locked trade once the gap clears the fees.

Professionals quoting whole ladders should close such gaps first. A vendor's scan found genuine bracket candidates "thin, longshot, and barely positive net of fees", and the 4¢ gap in its worked example turns into about −1¢ once each leg's taker fee is charged ([BotForKalshi](https://www.botforkalshi.com/blog/kalshi-arbitrage-guide)). Logging these gaps still costs nothing, and the test is fully honest.

Market-anchored pooling (strategy 10) is the best-documented job for an LLM. A blend with 0.2 weight on the LLM and 0.8 on the crowd improved the Brier score from .149 to .146 ([Halawi et al.](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/5a5acfd0876c940d81619c1dc60e7748-Paper-Conference.pdf)). Bridgewater's AIA Forecaster scored worse than liquid markets on its own (0.1258 against 0.1106), yet improved on them when given about a third of the weight ([AIA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.07678)). The gains are thousandths of a Brier point. The only claim of profit after fees, +80.33% in a month on Kalshi, comes from the benchmark's own team ([Gu, Kagan & Sun](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.06166)). LLMs gain ground against markets as the time to resolution grows, so this belongs in long-dated, text-heavy markets.

Long-dated favorites in fee-free series (strategy 11) rest on Intrade's mispricing of 4.7–10.9 points at prices of 20¢ and 80¢ more than 100 days out, which Page and Clemen explained as traders discounting a distant payout ([Page & Clemen](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1468-0297.2012.02561.x)). Gebele and Matthes find that the time value of money explains 48–88% of how near-certain contracts' discounts grow with time to settlement ([Gebele & Matthes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.31431)), so most of the discount is fair pay for locked capital. Any edge left over depends on Kalshi's interest on open positions letting a holder collect both the discount and the interest. That interest was 4.05% a year in an older announcement and has varied since ([Kalshi News](https://news.kalshi.com/p/interest-cash-open-positions)). No one has tested it.

The last four strategies need a second venue, an institutional setup or proprietary data. A cross-venue maker hybrid (strategy 12) rests orders where makers pay nothing and hedges on the other venue. But Polymarket US needs its own verified account, reportedly excludes Nevada, and pays maker rebates only above $10 million of monthly maker volume ([Polymarket US fees](https://docs.polymarket.us/fees); [Polymarket101](https://www.polymarket101.com/en/docs/guides/polymarket-us-vs-international-2026/)).

Combo market making (strategy 13) faces the most recreational money on Kalshi: combo takers reportedly lost more than $1 billion while combo makers made $519.8 million after fees ([InGame](https://www.ingame.com/kalshi-parlay-taker-losses-top-1b/)). It needs models of how the legs correlate, and a quoting setup that is unverified for small traders.

Slow cross-venue convergence (strategy 14) trades gaps that are real but mostly earned. Matching markets on different venues show lasting price gaps of 2–4% even after execution costs ([Gebele & Matthes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.01706)). One cross-venue Clarity Act trade's expected 5.08% fell to 2.67% after fees and the cost of tied-up capital ([Krause](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6984101)). One audit watched a "locked" arbitrage become a 74¢ loss when the two venues settled differently ([PredictMarketCap](https://predictmarketcap.com/analysis/prediction-market-arbitrage-study)).

A proprietary economic-data nowcast (strategy 15) would have to beat a crowd that already beats the Bloomberg consensus on headline CPI and ties it on core CPI and unemployment. The same crowd called the Fed's decision correctly the day before every meeting studied ([FEDS 2026-010](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2026010pap.pdf)). The nowcast would have only 4–12 releases a year per series to prove itself.

## About thirty rejected strategies face a calibrated price or a faster rival

The rejected strategies fail in one of three ways. They forecast against a price that is already better calibrated than their model, or they race professionals who are faster, or they hit a structural wall of fees, sportsbook account limits or contracts that settle differently. The cutoff is the counterparty test, not the score. That is why taking sharp-line prices in main sports markets is rejected even though it scores as high as the bottom of the viable list.

Forecasting against the price fails most clearly in the desk's own markets. Across 1,904 BTC and ETH 15-minute contracts from October 24 to November 3, the market's calibration slope from seven minutes to one minute before the close was **0.96–1.04 for BTC and 0.96–1.10 for ETH**. A slope of 1 is perfect, and above 1 means too close to 50/50. One minute out, the market's log loss (lower is better) was 0.183, against 0.253–0.284 for models based on spot prices ([Kalshi API](https://api.elections.kalshi.com/trade-api/v2/markets?series_ticker=KXBTC15M&status=settled)).

Others found the same. The best machine-learning correction improved log loss over the market price by 0.0014 on held-back data, with a confidence interval crossing zero ([AllAboutAI-YT](https://github.com/AllAboutAI-YT/kalshi-btc15-ml-experiment)). Odds implied by Deribit options lost about 1.38¢ a contract after fees ([edgebook](https://github.com/milesiscool/edgebook/pull/31)). Only 102 of 4,904 backtested BTC 15-minute strategies made money in one vendor's April sweep, and the April winner of the "panic-fade" family then lost when retested ([Turbine](https://www.turbinefi.com/blog/5000-strategy-backtest-kalshi-btc-15m)). Paper-trading agents, mostly on 15-minute crypto, lost 3.35¢ a contract after fees, against about 0.4¢ for a coin-flip control ([coloshword](https://github.com/coloshword/kalshi-15m-backtest)).

The one positive result in the researcher's own simulations, +5.5¢ to +6.8¢ a contract for BTC seven minutes out, was 1 of 84 overlapping tests over ten days. It vanished for ETH and at nearby horizons, the signature of a false positive.

The LLM evidence points the same way. Frontier models trading real money lost **16–30.8%** of their capital on Kalshi ([Prediction Arena](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.07355v1)). On live Kalshi events, markets beat LLMs as resolution approached, and even GPT-5 got back an average of 0.943 per dollar bet, below break-even ([Prophet Arena](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2026/file/aec5e2847c5ae90f939ab786774856cc-Paper-Conference.pdf)). An LLM choosing YES, NO or skip each minute lost $10.71 over 54 windows ([BotForKalshi](https://www.botforkalshi.com/blog/kalshi-bitcoin-bot)). The 2026 headlines about AI matching top human forecasters ([FRI](https://forecastingresearch.substack.com/p/ai-models-have-likely-reached-parity)) compare models with people, not with traded prices.

The speed races have real mechanisms and the wrong winners. Polymarket's 15-minute crypto markets supported profitable latency arbitrage in late 2025, until Polymarket added taker fees aimed at it ([Cointelegraph via TradingView](https://www.tradingview.com/news/cointelegraph:e59c32089094b:0-polymarket-quietly-introduces-taker-fees-on-15-minute-crypto-markets/)). Polymarket quotes now react to large Binance moves in a median 347 milliseconds ([OpenMarket](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.26245v1)).

On Kalshi, minute-by-minute data show quotes absorbing almost all of a spot move within the same minute, with a correlation of 0.695 to the same minute's move and 0.049 to the previous minute's. One snapshot showed more than 13,000 contracts resting at the best BTC bid ([Kalshi API](https://api.elections.kalshi.com/trade-api/v2/markets/KXBTC15M-26NOV031430-30/orderbook)).

Other races are just as fast. CME fed funds futures moved about 0.6–1.2 seconds ahead of Kalshi after the September CPI release ([BMLL](https://www.bmlltech.com/news/market-insight/when-kalshi-and-fed-funds-futures-disagree)). In-game sports price gaps last about six seconds ([sportstrades](https://github.com/VV1Git/sportstrades)), and gaps between venues on the same game a median nine ([Mustopo](https://dev.to/michaelmustopo/built-a-prediction-market-arbitrage-no-sizable-arbitrage-found-36cf)). A retail desk that checks prices every 12 seconds is the stale quote in every one of these races.

The structural walls are arithmetic. Paying two taker fees costs about 3.5¢ per $1 at mid prices, against a median real Kalshi-Polymarket gap of 0.78¢, and none of 12 headline arbitrages survived a one-day check that the quotes were current ([PredictMarketCap](https://predictmarketcap.com/analysis/prediction-market-arbitrage-study)). Buying every YES in a bracket pays a taker fee on every leg. Sportsbooks limit winning accounts, which is how they shut down consensus betting before ([Kaunitz et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.02824)). PredictIt takes 10% of profits and 5% of withdrawals ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PredictIt)).

## The desk's blend is a long-shot buying machine

The desk's code explains its losses. Its probability is a 50/50 average of a pricing formula and Jev's "settles YES" answer: p_yes = 0.5 × formula + 0.5 × Jev. The formula assumes prices move randomly with no drift.

The market price is not part of the blend; it enters only as the bar the blend has to clear. The desk buys YES when the blend beats the ask, or NO when 1 minus the blend beats 1 minus the bid, by 1.5 times the one-contract taker fee plus half the spread ([policy/binary.py](/home/user/Dealbot/policy/binary.py)). The formula prices the contract off the current Coinbase spot price, with volatility measured over 20 twelve-second blocks, about four minutes ([kalshi/model.py](/home/user/Dealbot/kalshi/model.py); [desk/config.py](/home/user/Dealbot/desk/config.py)). Jev sees the formula's output and the quotes before it answers ([questions/kalshi.py](/home/user/Dealbot/questions/kalshi.py)).

Nothing in the research supports a fixed linear blend that leaves the market out. Any weighted average of different forecasts is less calibrated and less sharp than it should be ([Ranjan & Gneiting](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jorssb:v:72:y:2010:i:1:p:71-91)), and random error pulls averaged probabilities toward 0.5 ([Baron et al.](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ordeca:v:11:y:2014:i:2:p:133-145)).

The researcher's simulation reproduces the desk's symptom exactly. Blending a four-minute-volatility spot model 50/50 with a forecast that carries no information halved its average distance from 50% one minute out, from 0.378 to 0.189, while the market's was 0.427. It raised log loss from 0.284 to 0.426, against the market's 0.183, and produced a calibration slope of 3.84, which is deeply underconfident ([Kalshi API](https://api.elections.kalshi.com/trade-api/v2/markets?series_ticker=KXBTC15M&status=settled)).

The desk's own 74 scored forecasts fit that picture. The market scored a Brier of 0.182, Jev 0.192 and the blend 0.196. Squared error is convex, so a 50/50 blend can score no worse than the average of its inputs. That means the formula scored at least 0.200 on the same forecasts, worse than both, and that its errors moved with Jev's at a correlation of roughly 0.78–1.0. Jev mostly restates the formula it is shown.

On 74 cases these gaps are only about 0.9–2.4 standard errors, so they suggest rather than prove. But they match the strong prior that the market wins near resolution. The desk can confirm them with a paired Diebold–Mariano test on its own logs, which compares two forecasters' errors case by case.

The trading consequence is a systematic tilt toward long shots. A forecast pulled toward 50% always rates the cheaper side as underpriced, by an amount that grows with the price's distance from 50¢. Pull halfway and a 10¢ contract looks like a 30% chance, a 20-point "edge" against a bar of about 2¢, while a 50¢ contract shows no edge at all. The bar is also lowest at the extremes, because the fee scales with P × (1 − P).

So the desk buys the cheapest contracts, which are the worst-returning trade on Kalshi for takers. In the desk's own markets, contracts priced 0–5¢ settled YES in none of 188 BTC cases one minute out and none of 68 ETH cases seven minutes out ([Kalshi API](https://api.elections.kalshi.com/trade-api/v2/markets/candlesticks)).

The desk's shadow comparison points the same way. Skipping entries more than 10 points from the market price would have lost **$6.30, against the desk's $20.17**, over the same hours ([reports/shadow.py](/home/user/Dealbot/reports/shadow.py)). That result is in-sample, but it repeats a pattern the-oracle found in weather. There, model signals priced at 10¢ or less won 3.0% of the time for a −53.4% return ([the-oracle](https://github.com/christurgeon/the-oracle/blob/main/docs/research/2026-07-19-new-data-edge-audit.md)). Choosing trades by the size of the model-market gap picked out exactly the trades the market was right about ([the-oracle](https://github.com/christurgeon/the-oracle/blob/main/docs/research/2026-05-30-weather-edge-search-dossier.md)). Disagreement with a calibrated price is mostly model error.

The formula has three fixable flaws beyond the blend. First, it assumes settlement on a single spot price, but the contract settles on a 60-second average. That removes about 40 seconds of uncertainty before the final minute and most of it inside. Pricing the average cut BTC log loss one minute out from 0.269 to 0.253, and moved the calibration slope from 1.85 to 0.99 ([Kalshi API](https://api.elections.kalshi.com/trade-api/v2/markets/candlesticks)).

Second, it uses Coinbase rather than the settlement index. Coinbase's last trade differed from the settlement average by a median 1.42 basis points for BTC and 1.72 for ETH. It ran about 1 basis point low and called the wrong outcome in 5.57% and 4.73% of markets respectively ([Coinbase candles](https://api.exchange.coinbase.com/products/BTC-USD/candles)).

The desk has already hit this. Its provisional rule called KXETH15M-26NOV022215-15 NO, at a Coinbase price of 2249.82 against a 2249.99 strike. Kalshi's 60-second average was 2250.08, and the contract settled YES ([tests/test_kalshi.py](/home/user/Dealbot/tests/test_kalshi.py)).

Third, four-minute volatility is noisy and usually low, at a median 0.72–0.89 of 60-minute volatility. That makes the formula on its own too extreme mid-window, even as the blend drags the combination toward 50%. Kalshi now streams the settlement index itself, including trailing 60-second averages, over its authenticated WebSocket ([Kalshi docs](https://docs.kalshi.com/websockets/cfbenchmarks-value)).

| Flaw in the desk | What the evidence says | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| The market price is not part of the forecast; it is only the bar to clear ([policy/binary.py](/home/user/Dealbot/policy/binary.py)) | The market price is calibrated in the desk's trading window (slopes of 0.96–1.04 for BTC and 0.96–1.10 for ETH, 7 to 1 minutes out) and beat every model tested | Start from the market price: q = m + w × (model − m), where m is the market price and w is refit on each new stretch of data and expected to be near zero |
| Jev carries half the weight (`BINARY_MODEL_WEIGHT` = 0.5) without demonstrated skill | A no-skill forecast in a linear blend just pulls toward 50%; the simulated 50/50 blend raised log loss from 0.284 to 0.426, against the market's 0.183 | Set Jev's weight to zero until 2,000–3,000 resolved paired forecasts show an improvement on fresh data; keep it only as a logged veto |
| The formula prices off a single spot price at expiry ([kalshi/model.py](/home/user/Dealbot/kalshi/model.py)) and ignores the 60-second settlement average | Remaining variance is σ² × (time left − 40 seconds) before the final minute and far smaller inside it; pricing the average fixed calibration one minute out (slope 1.85 to 0.99) and cut log loss | Use time left minus 40 seconds, the partial-average formula in the last minute, or Kalshi's own trailing 60-second index average |
| Spot comes from Coinbase, not the CF Benchmarks index | Median gap of 1.4–1.7 basis points, running about 1 basis point low; last-trade calls were wrong in 5.6% of BTC and 4.7% of ETH markets | Subscribe to Kalshi's `cfbenchmarks_value` feed or its five-a-second version; failing that, apply a rolling correction for the gap |
| Volatility is measured over 20 twelve-second blocks, about 4 minutes ([desk/config.py](/home/user/Dealbot/desk/config.py)) | Noisy and usually low (median 0.72–0.89 of 60-minute volatility), which makes the formula too extreme mid-window (slope 0.59–0.79) | A 30–120-minute weighted moving average scaled by time of day and allowing for fat tails, or the volatility implied by the market price |
| Jev is graded on Brier score against the base rate ([ops/analyst_prompt.md](/home/user/Dealbot/ops/analyst_prompt.md)) | Against a base rate, a model that simply echoes the market price looks skilled | Grade every forecaster against the market price at the same moment with a paired test, and report P&L by price bucket and by side |

Fixing all six brings the model up to roughly the market's calibration and no further. Even the corrected averaging-aware model lost to the market price one minute out: 0.253 against 0.183 for BTC, and 0.225 against 0.166 for ETH. The blunt implication is that a correctly anchored taker model in a calibrated market should almost never trade, and every extra trade is the model's own error placed as an order.

The corrected model is worth most as the pricing engine for post-only quotes in series with no maker fee, which is strategy 4. Jev's best remaining use is as a logged veto for information the price cannot hold, such as a scheduled economic release inside the window or a data-feed outage. Its value should be judged by comparing the paper P&L of vetoed and allowed trades. Its "pickoff" question, which asks whether the ask is stale, is a millisecond problem that code answers better than a model that takes seconds. Nothing should be pushed toward the extremes either, because Jev's information largely overlaps the market's.

Plan sample sizes before trusting any result. A contract near 50¢ pays $1 or $0, so its payoff varies by about 50¢ either way. Detecting an edge at two standard errors therefore takes about 10,000 independent trades for a 1¢ edge, about 2,500 for 2¢ and about 400 for 5¢. At one trade per window per series, the desk's 192 windows a day give 2,500 trades in about two weeks and 10,000 in about seven. Correlation between BTC and ETH windows stretches both timelines. With only 74 forecasts, a fitted weight for Jev would carry a standard error near 0.6.

## Paper can falsify maker strategies but never confirm them

Strategies split cleanly by how a paper fill is decided. A taker fill is mechanical: take the displayed ask for no more than the displayed size and charge the exact fee. The paper result is then close to what live trading would have produced, as long as quotes carry real timestamps and the test adds a realistic delay. One builder's rule is to rerun every result at two to three times the measured delay, since "an edge that disappears at 300ms is a latency race you'll lose live" ([kalshi-lab](https://github.com/RishabKalluri/kalshi-lab)).

A fill on a resting order is not mechanical. A paper simulation that sees only the quote cannot know the order's place in the queue, or whether the price touching the order would actually have filled it. Nor can it reproduce the way fills arrive disproportionately just before the price moves against the maker. So it overstates both how often a maker is filled and how good the fills are. The makers' profits in the Kalshi studies belong to firms at the front of the queue, and the best-documented weather bot did about 3.3 times worse live than in simulation ([the-oracle](https://github.com/christurgeon/the-oracle/blob/main/docs/research/2026-07-19-new-data-edge-audit.md)). Paper P&L for a maker is a ceiling, and the real figure can be a loss.

On that basis, three groups of strategies cannot be tested honestly on paper. The first is every maker strategy: the fill side of liquidity-incentive capture (1), maker long-shot fading (2), sports, crypto and weather market making (3, 4, 5), the cross-venue maker hybrid (12) and combo market making (13). The second is every speed race in the rejected list, whose outcome depends on live contests with faster takers and on how fast market makers cancel. The third is every cross-venue taker trade, which risks filling one leg without the other, something paper cannot see.

A further group can be logged honestly but not confirmed on any useful timescale, because its main risk shows up only at settlement or its events are rare. That group is long-dated favorites (11), slow cross-venue convergence (14), pairs within consistency arbitrage (9) that settle on different facts, and economic-data nowcasts (15).

The honest paper tests are the taker strategies: thin-market sports anchoring (7), modeling the TWC settlement figure (6), late-window tail harvesting (8), spotting consistency-arbitrage violations (9) and the accuracy side of market-anchored pooling (10). The rejected taker strategies are honestly testable too, which is how anyone knows they lose. Honest does not mean fast: strategies that buy near-certain favorites need roughly 4,000–8,000 independent settled bets to detect an edge of a few tenths of a point.

Maker strategies can still be bounded on paper if the bound is built pessimistically. That means recording every change to the full order book, and every trade print, from the authenticated WebSocket rather than just the best quotes. It means assuming each order joins the back of the queue at its price, and counting a fill only after traded volume at that price exceeds the displayed size ahead of it, or only when a trade prints through the price. It means measuring markouts, how the price moves after each fill, at one minute, five minutes and settlement, to put a number on losses to informed traders. And it means estimating incentive rewards separately, from one-second full-depth snapshots and Kalshi's published formula.

The bound then decides whether to run a tiny live pilot: a few contracts per order with a hard loss cap. The pilot reads Kalshi's queue-position endpoint ([Kalshi changelog](https://docs.kalshi.com/changelog)) and tunes the fill model against reality. Only that pilot shows whether the strategy makes or loses money.

The desk's current data cannot support maker shadows. Every 12 seconds, its tape records each contract's best YES bid and ask, quote time, strike, Coinbase spot, recent volatility and the formula's probability, with no displayed sizes and no trade prints ([desk/desk.py](/home/user/Dealbot/desk/desk.py)). The feed polls only the best bid and ask ([kalshi/feed.py](/home/user/Dealbot/kalshi/feed.py)). The existing shadow report can only skip or exit trades the desk itself took, never open one ([reports/shadow.py](/home/user/Dealbot/reports/shadow.py)). The tape does let new taker rules be replayed on every block, though 12 seconds is too coarse for the final minute.

Every shadow below needs the same upgrade: Kalshi's authenticated WebSocket, carrying order-book changes, trades and the CF Benchmarks index, plus displayed sizes on the tape. ## Conclusion

The research turns the desk's question around. On Kalshi the edge is not a probability that beats the price. It is a position in the order book and in the rulebook: the maker's place in the queue, the incentive formula, the 60-second settlement average, and a fee that shrinks toward the extremes. That gives the desk's work a use it does not have today. A corrected pricing model that only matches the market is worthless as a taker signal, yet it is exactly the quoting engine a maker strategy needs. The LLM earns a place only as a logged veto on information no price can hold. A desk that trades often against a calibrated market is trading its own errors; a correct one would mostly wait.

The harder lesson is about how to decide. Confidence and testability run in opposite directions here. The strategies most likely to win rest orders, and resting orders are the one thing paper cannot fill honestly. The strategies paper judges cleanly are the ones least likely to pay. A shadow test can therefore kill a maker strategy but never clear one, so the decision to go live has to rest on mechanism plus a few hundred dollars of live fills with markouts. And because the top edges are partly Kalshi policy, the ranking is perishable: the incentive program was amended three times this year, a volume program just ended, and maker fees are being added series by series. It should be re-scored whenever Kalshi's fee-change log or incentive-program list changes.
